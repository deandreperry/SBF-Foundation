01 / WEAR YOUR SUPPORT

The design challenge

Make belonging visible.
Make help discoverable.

SBF supports people and families impacted by lupus and fibromyalgia. Its website needs to welcome people seeking help and people looking for a meaningful way to contribute.

This case study focuses on two parts of the broader redesign: Custom Apparel and the Resource Center.

01 / THE CHALLENGE

One mission.
Two very different moments.

A visitor browsing a custom jersey can spend time exploring identity, style, and personalization. Someone looking for prescription assistance may need a useful destination quickly. A consistent brand should serve both situations without forcing them into the same page structure.

My design approach connects those experiences through a recognizable visual language while giving each a distinct hierarchy: product discovery for apparel, topic-based navigation through information for resources.

“I want to show my support.”

Help a supporter recognize the collection, compare styles, understand custom ordering, and continue to the store.

“I need help finding support.”

Help a visitor understand the available information, identify a relevant service category, and reach an organization or SBF contact.

These are design framing statements, not quotations from research participants. No interview or usability-study findings are claimed.

Guiding principle

The brand should create recognition.
The hierarchy should create direction.

02 / CUSTOM APPAREL

Give merchandise a role
in the mission.

The apparel experience presents the collection as a way to express identity and solidarity. A large jacket image gives the page a tangible focal point, while the headline “Wear Your Strength. Represent the Fight.” ties the product to SBF’s purpose.

The commercial and emotional messages have separate jobs: the hero establishes the collection’s meaning; the category index and catalog supply concrete choices.

01

Start with recognition.

A product-led hero, strong purple field, and direct collection CTA establish what the page offers. The artwork remains the focal point; supporting copy explains its connection to awareness and community.

Tradeoff: the extended mission narrative adds meaning but also delays the catalog. Its position is a candidate for task-based testing.

02

Make the catalog legible.

Six visual categories—baseball, football, basketball, hockey, hoodies, and jackets—let visitors begin with a familiar clothing type. Square image areas and repeating card anatomy create consistency across the collection.

Tradeoff: a category-first model supports browsing. A visitor who already wants a specific item may need a more direct product link.

03

Explain the commitment.

Product cards expose price, name, description, and a category CTA. Native expandable sections explain returns, shipping, sizing, and customization; the return policy is expanded by default.

Tradeoff: policies sit late in a long page. Critical sizing and personalization guidance should also be verified at the actual purchase decision.

Implemented handoff

  1. Understand the collection
  2. Choose a category
  3. Browse the SBF store

The scope shown here is discovery and the store handoff. A product configurator, cart, and checkout were not built in these static pages.

03 / RESOURCE CENTER

Structure information
around recognizable needs.

The Resource Center brings education and practical assistance into one destination. Rather than presenting every link as equally important, it groups the material into sections with explicit headings, consistent cards, and visible destinations.

The page distinguishes condition education from community resources, then provides support and contact routes. This creates an inspectable structure that can be maintained as organizations and programs change.

01

Use a predictable reading pattern.

Lupus and fibromyalgia education use parallel structures: overview, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment or management, and downloadable material. Repetition is intended to make the content easier to orient within.

Next validation: confirm that the reading level, terminology, and amount of detail meet visitors’ needs. Clinical content needs an accountable review process.

02

Name the practical need.

Healthcare, prescription assistance, food assistance, and financial assistance are distinct categories. Prescription support is further separated into assistance programs, savings programs, and government or insurance help.

Next validation: ask participants to find help for a specific situation and observe whether these labels match their expectations.

03

Make the destination visible.

Resource cards display organization names, domains, and, where available, a short description or geographic context. A contact panel offers another route when visitors are unsure which service to choose.

Next validation: review links for destination specificity. An organization homepage may still leave a visitor searching for the actual service.

A consequential information architecture tradeoff

Education comes first. Urgent intent may not.

The current page places lengthy education sections before practical assistance and crisis support. A next iteration should test a concise “What do you need?” jump menu near the top, with a clearly discoverable urgent-support route. That would preserve the information while reducing the amount of scrolling needed to reach it.

Proposed next iteration—not a feature claimed in the current implementation.

04 / VISUAL SYSTEM

Consistency without
making every page identical.

The common foundation is Poppins typography, SBF purple, soft lavender surfaces, dark body text, rounded cards, and pill-shaped actions. Apparel gives imagery more space and uses the brand color for expression. Resources uses the same identity to separate sections and identify actions.

Light lavender#F7F1FB

The reusable unit is the decision, not just the rectangle.

A merchandise card answers “What does it look like, what does it cost, and where do I browse?” A resource card answers “Who provides this service, what is it for, and where will I go?” Both use consistent hierarchy, but their content reflects different visitor decisions.

Responsive behavior in the source

Adapt the density
to the task.

The apparel catalog changes from three columns to two at 980px and one at 680px. The Resource Center stacks its grids at 860px, giving longer organization names and descriptive text more room.

Semantic headings, real links, focus-state styles, and native disclosure elements provide useful foundations. They are implementation choices, not a claim of complete accessibility conformance.

Accessibility review priorities

  • Keyboard order and visible focus across every interactive element.
  • Text contrast on gradients and light-colored surfaces.
  • Long labels, zoom, and narrow-screen wrapping.
  • Reduced-motion handling for hover transforms.
  • Clear external destinations and accessible downloadable documents.
05 / OUTCOMES & ACCOUNTABILITY

Be precise about
what the work demonstrates.

The implementation demonstrates two distinct page architectures using a consistent brand: a six-category apparel discovery experience and a Resource Center covering education and four practical assistance categories. Both include responsive layouts and clear handoffs to existing services.

There is no supplied analytics baseline, moderated study, or verified conversion report for these pages. I would not describe the redesign as having increased revenue, reduced support demand, or improved task completion without measuring those outcomes.

Delivered

Visual hierarchy, responsive page implementations, catalog organization, resource grouping, and explicit action paths.

To measure

Whether people find the right next step more successfully—and whether the handoff works once they leave the redesigned page.

Reflection

Designing for a mission
means designing the next step.

The most meaningful part of this work is the relationship between expression and usefulness. The apparel experience gives supporters a visible way to participate. The Resource Center gives visitors structured routes toward information and assistance.

The next stage is to validate those routes with people and improve the handoffs. A polished interface is the beginning of that responsibility, not evidence that the entire service experience is solved.
